## 2026-09-24 — Tokenizer files rebuilt from design-chat notes

The syllable tokenizer (`syllabify.py`, `tokenizer.py`, `eval_hybrid.py`,
`tests/test_syllabify.py`) that was built and locked in during the design
chat was **not present** in this repo when execution started (repo had only
an empty `.git`). Per the execution brief, I was to ask before proceeding —
asked Navid, who chose to have me **rebuild the tokenizer from the detailed
design-chat notes** rather than wait for the original files or a re-upload.

Rebuild is faithful to the documented design:
- CMUdict (majority vote across pronunciations when they disagree on
  syllable count — this was one of the two locked-in fixes) gives the
  syllable count for in-dictionary words.
- A DP over candidate cut positions places the count-1 cut points, scored by
  agreement with pyphen's hyphenation, agreement with NLTK's Sonority
  Sequencing Principle (SSP) syllable splitter, a legal-English-onset bonus,
  and small common-suffix bonuses.
- OOV words (not in CMUdict) get their count from a fallback: majority vote
  of {vowel-group heuristic, pyphen count, NLTK SSP count}, then the same
  DP places cuts.
- Special-casing (the second locked-in fix): contractions split their
  clitic (`n't`, `'s`, `'d`, `'ll`, `'re`, `'ve`, `'m`) off as a separate
  final piece; word-final consonant+"le" (table, cradle, …) is treated as a
  syllabic-consonant nucleus on its own.
- First syllable/piece of each word is marked with a leading "▁"
  (U+2581); punctuation characters and single digits are standalone tokens;
  everything is lowercased.

**Caveat, worth carrying into the write-up**: this is a reimplementation
from notes, not the literal original code. Re-running `eval_hybrid.py` on
Brown reproduced the documented statistics closely (syllables/word 1.555 vs.
noted ~1.55; chars/syllable-token 3.03 vs. noted ~3.0; top-2k/4k/8k coverage
91.3%/96.7%/99.3% vs. noted 90.7%/96.1%/98.9%; pieces always rejoin the word,
100%; vowel-in-every-piece 98.35% vs. noted 98.9%), so the rebuild is judged
close enough in character to proceed without re-deriving the whole design.
Exact per-word boundary placements will differ in some cases from whatever
the original code produced — this only matters if a future comparison
depends on bit-identical tokenization to the original design-chat
prototype, which nothing in the experiment plan requires (the vocabulary is
being rebuilt from WIT3 anyway, not reused from Brown).

Full comparison numbers are in `eval_hybrid_report.json` (regenerated by
this rebuild) — see `results/eval_hybrid_report_summary.md` for a copy of
the headline numbers alongside the original notes' numbers.

## 2026-09-24 — Hardware and throughput benchmark

Training runs execute via the same sandboxed Linux VM this session uses to
work on the connected `language-cadence-2` folder (4 logical CPUs exposed,
Intel Core i5-1038NG7 @ 2.00GHz, ~3.8GB RAM available to the VM, CPU only —
no CUDA/MPS acceleration available here).

Ran `scripts/benchmark_throughput.py`: a tiny GPT at the likely sweep
architecture (D=64, 4 layers, 4 heads, seq_len=384, vocab=8000, batch=16;
~1.25M params) for 100s of steady-state training (forward+backward+AdamW
step, no dataloader/IO overhead included). Result: **361 ms/step, ~17,000
tokens/sec**.

Feasibility against the 48h total-compute budget: at this throughput, 48h
buys ~479,000 total training steps. One "roughly one epoch" run over the
~4-5M-word WIT3 corpus at this batch/seq config is on the order of 2,500-
3,500 steps (word count -> syllable-token count still to be confirmed once
the corpus is parsed). Even a generous experiment plan — say 14 periods x
3 seeds x ~3000 steps, plus a handful of controls (wrong-period, learned-
phase, RoPE) — comes to well under 200,000 steps, i.e. well under 24h of
raw training compute. **48h budget looks comfortably feasible**; there is
headroom to add a second seed pass, a slightly larger model, or optional
extras (written-text control, BNC2014 transfer test) without threatening
the budget. IO/preprocessing overhead and real-corpus dataloading will add
some wall-clock on top of pure training time, but not enough to change this
conclusion.

Caveat: this benchmark measures the sandboxed VM's throughput, which is
where actual training will run (via the same device-bridge shell used for
this session) — it is not a bare-metal macOS benchmark, and full-machine
performance could differ if training is instead run directly on macOS.
