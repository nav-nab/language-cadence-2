## 2026-09-24 — Tokenizer files rebuilt from design-chat notes

The syllable tokenizer (`syllabify.py`, `tokenizer.py`, `eval_hybrid.py`,
`tests/test_syllabify.py`) that was built and locked in during the design
chat was **not present** in this repo when execution started (repo had only
an empty `.git`). Per the execution brief, I was to ask before proceeding —
asked Navid, who chose to have me **rebuild the tokenizer from the detailed
design-chat notes** rather than wait for the original files or a re-upload.

Rebuild is faithful to the documented design:
- CMUdict (majority vote across pronunciations when they disagree on
  syllable count — this was one of the two locked-in fixes) gives the
  syllable count for in-dictionary words.
- A DP over candidate cut positions places the count-1 cut points, scored by
  agreement with pyphen's hyphenation, agreement with NLTK's Sonority
  Sequencing Principle (SSP) syllable splitter, a legal-English-onset bonus,
  and small common-suffix bonuses.
- OOV words (not in CMUdict) get their count from a fallback: majority vote
  of {vowel-group heuristic, pyphen count, NLTK SSP count}, then the same
  DP places cuts.
- Special-casing (the second locked-in fix): contractions split their
  clitic (`n't`, `'s`, `'d`, `'ll`, `'re`, `'ve`, `'m`) off as a separate
  final piece; word-final consonant+"le" (table, cradle, …) is treated as a
  syllabic-consonant nucleus on its own.
- First syllable/piece of each word is marked with a leading "▁"
  (U+2581); punctuation characters and single digits are standalone tokens;
  everything is lowercased.

**Caveat, worth carrying into the write-up**: this is a reimplementation
from notes, not the literal original code. Re-running `eval_hybrid.py` on
Brown reproduced the documented statistics closely (syllables/word 1.555 vs.
noted ~1.55; chars/syllable-token 3.03 vs. noted ~3.0; top-2k/4k/8k coverage
91.3%/96.7%/99.3% vs. noted 90.7%/96.1%/98.9%; pieces always rejoin the word,
100%; vowel-in-every-piece 98.35% vs. noted 98.9%), so the rebuild is judged
close enough in character to proceed without re-deriving the whole design.
Exact per-word boundary placements will differ in some cases from whatever
the original code produced — this only matters if a future comparison
depends on bit-identical tokenization to the original design-chat
prototype, which nothing in the experiment plan requires (the vocabulary is
being rebuilt from WIT3 anyway, not reused from Brown).

Full comparison numbers are in `eval_hybrid_report.json` (regenerated by
this rebuild) — see `results/eval_hybrid_report_summary.md` for a copy of
the headline numbers alongside the original notes' numbers.

## 2026-09-24 — Hardware and throughput benchmark

Training runs execute via the same sandboxed Linux VM this session uses to
work on the connected `language-cadence-2` folder (4 logical CPUs exposed,
Intel Core i5-1038NG7 @ 2.00GHz, ~3.8GB RAM available to the VM, CPU only —
no CUDA/MPS acceleration available here).

Ran `scripts/benchmark_throughput.py`: a tiny GPT at the likely sweep
architecture (D=64, 4 layers, 4 heads, seq_len=384, vocab=8000, batch=16;
~1.25M params) for 100s of steady-state training (forward+backward+AdamW
step, no dataloader/IO overhead included). Result: **361 ms/step, ~17,000
tokens/sec**.

Feasibility against the 48h total-compute budget: at this throughput, 48h
buys ~479,000 total training steps. One "roughly one epoch" run over the
~4-5M-word WIT3 corpus at this batch/seq config is on the order of 2,500-
3,500 steps (word count -> syllable-token count still to be confirmed once
the corpus is parsed). Even a generous experiment plan — say 14 periods x
3 seeds x ~3000 steps, plus a handful of controls (wrong-period, learned-
phase, RoPE) — comes to well under 200,000 steps, i.e. well under 24h of
raw training compute. **48h budget looks comfortably feasible**; there is
headroom to add a second seed pass, a slightly larger model, or optional
extras (written-text control, BNC2014 transfer test) without threatening
the budget. IO/preprocessing overhead and real-corpus dataloading will add
some wall-clock on top of pure training time, but not enough to change this
conclusion.

Caveat: this benchmark measures the sandboxed VM's throughput, which is
where actual training will run (via the same device-bridge shell used for
this session) — it is not a bare-metal macOS benchmark, and full-machine
performance could differ if training is instead run directly on macOS.

## 2026-09-24 — Preprocessing frozen and applied

Checked with Navid; punctuation handling was reconsidered (see below) and
everything else confirmed as proposed. Frozen preprocessing, applied by
`scripts/preprocess_wit3.py`:

1. **Cue stripping**: parenthetical audience/audio cues (`(Laughter)`,
   `(Applause)`, `(Music)`, `(In Chinese)`, etc., regex
   `\([A-Za-z][A-Za-z '\-]{1,40}\)`) are removed and surrounding whitespace
   collapsed. Dropped outright rather than replaced with a placeholder
   token.
2. **Minimum talk length**: drop talks under 100 words, measured AFTER cue
   stripping (so a talk that's mostly cues doesn't survive on cue-word
   count alone). 11 talks dropped (one more than the pre-cleaning estimate
   of 10, since removing cue words like "Applause"/"Laughter" pushed one
   additional talk below the threshold).
3. **Dedupe by talkid**: kept as a safety check in the pipeline; this
   release has zero duplicates, so it's a no-op here.
4. **Casing**: kept as original (not lowercased upstream) — the syllable
   tokenizer already lowercases internally, so there's no information lost
   either way, and no reason to lowercase the stored/committed-config-
   adjacent corpus text unnecessarily.
5. **Punctuation**: kept, reversing my initial lean toward dropping it.
   Punctuation is 10.1% of the token stream on a 150-talk sample (comma
   7.6pp of that on its own), which is nontrivial, but Navid's call was to
   keep it: commas/periods plausibly stand in for clause/sentence-boundary
   pauses in this transcribed-speech corpus, which is closer to signal than
   noise for a project whose original framing (see
   language-cadence-research) was about rhythmic/timing structure, even
   though the current framing is "training efficiency" specifically. Worth
   carrying into the write-up as a documented, deliberate choice with a
   stated tradeoff (easier-to-predict punctuation tokens do dilute the
   pure syllable-prediction signal somewhat), not a default.
6. **Digits**: kept as-is; the tokenizer already splits them into
   single-digit tokens.
7. **Train/val/test split**: by SPEAKER, not by talk. Talks are grouped by
   speaker, speaker groups sorted by total word count descending, and each
   group greedily assigned whole to whichever split has the largest
   remaining deficit against its target word-count share (90/5/5). Result:
   train 1667 talks / 3,893,364 words (90.00% by word share) / 1363
   speakers; val 202 talks / 216,272 words (5.00%) / 197 speakers; test 205
   talks / 216,365 words (5.00%) / 198 speakers. Verified zero speaker
   overlap between any pair of splits. Full report in
   `results/wit3_preprocess_report.json`.

Output: `data/processed/{train,val,test}.jsonl`, one record per talk
(talkid, speaker, date, title, cleaned content, word count). The
intermediate unfiltered `data/processed/talks.jsonl` is regenerable by
`scripts/parse_wit3.py` and not needed going forward.

## 2026-09-24 — Stage 4 implementation: model, PE, training loop

`src/positional_encoding.py`: `sinusoidal_pe` (fixed Vaswani-style base),
`periodic_pe` (v1's folded-harmonic construction, H=D/2 row-pairs,
k_j = min(j mod P, P-(j mod P))), `combined_pe` (base + periodic, or base
alone when period=None -- the no-periodic-component baseline).

`src/model.py`: `GPT` supports three `pe_mode`s: `"periodic"` and
`"baseline"` both use concat injection (`Linear(concat(token_emb, PE))`,
no amplitude hyperparameter needed); `"rope"` instead applies rotary
embeddings to queries/keys inside attention, with no additive/concat PE at
all. `tests/test_model.py` (11 tests) checks PE correctness (periodicity,
folded-harmonic formula, baseline-equals-sinusoidal), forward/backward
passes for all three modes, and confirms the frozen architecture lands at
~2.87M params as estimated (2,874,240 for periodic/baseline; 2,841,344 for
rope, which has no concat_proj layer).

Encoded the corpus to flat token-id arrays for training:
`scripts/encode_corpus_chunk.py` + `scripts/finalize_encoded_corpus.py`
(chunked, same reasoning as the vocab-building step) -> `data/processed/
{train,val,test}_ids.npy` (uint16; talks separated by `<eos>`). Train:
6,520,940 ids; val: 365,439; test: 366,465.

**Real benchmark of the frozen architecture** (`scripts/benchmark_real_arch.py`,
D=128/L4/H4/N=1024/vocab=8000/batch=16), replacing the earlier analytical
FLOP estimate:

| mode | step time | 3000-step run |
|---|---|---|
| periodic | 1.70s | 1.42h |
| baseline | 1.87s | 1.56h |
| rope | 1.83s | 1.52h |

Faster than the analytical ~2.16h/run estimate -- full plan (24 core-sweep
runs + 3 baseline + 3 RoPE control runs) now estimated at ~43.4h, under the
48h budget but with little margin once data loading/eval overhead is added.

**Memory finding**: peak RSS 3.1-3.4GB during the benchmark, against the
sandboxed VM's ~3.8GB ceiling -- a real OOM risk if training ran in this
VM. Resolved by Navid's plan to run the actual training loop on his own
machine (outside the sandbox) rather than in this Cowork session's VM,
which almost certainly has more headroom (the VM is a constrained sandbox,
not the real hardware). Batch size is a config value in `scripts/train.py`
so it can be adjusted if real-machine memory still turns out to be tight.

**Epoch-count note**: at N=1024 (vs v1's 384), the ~6.52M-token training
set gives only ~6362 non-overlapping (1025-token) windows, so 3000 steps x
batch 16 is roughly 7-8 passes over the training data, not "~1 epoch" as
the original protocol sketch assumed for a different architecture. This
affects what the "roughly one epoch / mid-budget / end" checkpoint
timing (from the brief's suggested protocol) should actually mean here --
flagged for Stage 5 (experiment protocol), not yet resolved.

**`scripts/train.py`**: resumable single-run trainer (one pe_mode/period/
seed combination), built to run on Navid's own machine.
- Data: non-overlapping (seq_len+1)-token windows over the encoded corpus,
  shuffled per epoch via a seed that's a pure function of (--seed, epoch)
  (`numpy.random.RandomState(seed*100000 + epoch)`), so the shuffle order
  never needs to be persisted -- resuming only needs (epoch, position-in-
  epoch), which the checkpoint does store. This is the "more standard"
  epoch-based approach v1 flagged as preferable to sampling-with-replacement.
- Checkpointing is WALL-CLOCK-timed (`--checkpoint_every_seconds`, default
  600s = 10 min), not step-count-timed, per Navid's requirement that an
  interruption cost at most 30 minutes regardless of a given machine/
  config's actual step speed. Checkpoints are written atomically (temp
  file + `os.replace`). Re-running the identical command resumes
  automatically -- no separate `--resume` flag.
- A checkpoint's config is checked against the new invocation's args on
  resume, but only for the subset that affects model/data/determinism
  (pe_mode, period, seed, batch_size, seq_len, d_model, n_layers, n_heads,
  lr, vocab_path, data_dir) -- `--steps`, `--eval_every_steps`, etc. are
  deliberately excluded so extending a run's step budget doesn't get
  wrongly rejected as a "mismatch" (an actual bug caught by the test suite
  below and fixed before this was committed).
- Metrics (train loss + cheap val-subset loss) are appended to
  `metrics.jsonl` incrementally, so progress survives an interruption even
  between checkpoints. A full test-set evaluation runs once at the end.
- `tests/test_train_resume.py`: functional test (tiny synthetic corpus/
  vocab, real subprocess invocations of the actual script) proving an
  interrupted-then-resumed run (steps 0->3, then 3->6) produces
  bit-identical final model weights and test loss to a straight-through
  0->6 run, with no duplicate/missing metrics entries. Caught two real
  bugs before they could have cost real compute time: (1) `.view()` on a
  non-contiguous tensor in the loss computation at small dimensions
  (fixed with `.reshape()`); (2) the initial config-mismatch check
  compared ALL args including `--steps`, which would have refused to
  resume a run being extended to a longer step budget -- fixed to check
  only the identity-critical subset above.

Full test suite: 48/48 passing.

## 2026-09-24 — Stage 5: experiment protocol

Confirmed with Navid (16GB RAM on his machine -- no memory concerns for
batch_size=16 as configured):

- **Learning rate**: fixed at 3e-4 for all runs, no per-config grid (the
  budget is already tight; a grid would multiply the run count). If a
  specific run visibly diverges it gets restarted by hand with a lower
  --lr, not swept automatically.
- **Seeds**: the same 3 seeds (0, 1, 2) are reused across every pe_mode/
  period combination (paired comparison, not independent samples), rather
  than fresh random seeds per config.
- **Overfitting / epoch-count handling**: resolves the Stage 4 finding
  that 3000 steps at N=1024 is ~7-8 epochs over the 6.5M-token training
  set, not "~1 epoch". `train.py` now tracks val loss throughout training
  and saves a second checkpoint (`checkpoint_best.pt`) whenever it
  improves; the reported final_test_loss for every run comes from that
  best-val checkpoint, not the final step's weights. best_val_loss/
  best_step are persisted in the main checkpoint so they survive resume.
- **Period selection on validation, not test**: the headline "does
  periodic PE help" comparison picks the best P by validation loss, then
  reports that P's test loss against baseline and RoPE. Test loss for all
  8 periods is still reported/plotted for the full curve.
- **Primary deliverable is a graph, not a single number**: Navid wants to
  see the shape of test loss vs. P across all 8 periods (monotonic
  decrease toward N as in language-cadence-1, a sweet spot, non-monotonic
  noise, etc.), with baseline and RoPE as reference lines -- the best-P
  comparison is one specific readout from that graph, not the only
  output.
- **Runs**: 8 periods x 3 seeds + 3 baseline seeds + 3 RoPE seeds = 30
  runs total (unchanged from Stage 4's budget estimate, ~43.4h).

**Implementation**:

`scripts/train.py` additions:
- `checkpoint_best.pt`: saved (atomically) whenever val_loss_subset
  improves during training; final test-set eval loads this checkpoint's
  weights instead of the final step's. `final_result.json` now also
  records pe_mode/period/seed/best_step/best_val_loss directly (not just
  run_id), for easier downstream aggregation.
- `check_finite_loss()`: hard halt (exit code 1, writes divergence.json)
  the moment a step's loss goes non-finite, *before* backward()/
  optimizer.step() can further corrupt the model -- the last checkpoint
  stays valid, so the fix is to restart that one run (fresh --out_dir or
  delete its checkpoint.pt) with a lower --lr, not to resume it as-is.
- `check_loss_spike()`: soft warning (logged to warnings.log + stderr,
  not fatal) when a step's loss is >10x the recent EMA after a 50-step
  warmup -- surfaces a suspicious run without halting it, since early
  training is naturally noisy.

`tests/test_train_protocol.py` (6 tests): unit tests for
`check_finite_loss` (halts on nan/inf, writes diagnostic, no-ops on a
finite value) and integration tests (via real subprocess runs of
train.py on a tiny synthetic corpus) proving `checkpoint_best.pt` and
`final_result.json`'s best_step/best_val_loss match the actual minimum
of the logged `metrics.jsonl` val_loss_subset values, including across
an interrupt/resume boundary.

`scripts/run_sweep.py`: launches all 30 runs by invoking train.py per
config. Skips any run whose `final_result.json` already exists, so
re-running the whole sweep after a partial interruption (beyond what
train.py's own per-run resume already handles) doesn't redo finished
runs. A diverged run (non-zero exit) is logged and the sweep continues
with the next config rather than aborting. `tests/test_run_sweep.py` (3
tests) checks the config list is exactly 8x3 periodic + 3 baseline + 3
RoPE with no duplicates, run_id formatting, and that --dry_run doesn't
actually invoke train.py.

`scripts/analyze_sweep.py`: aggregates `runs/*/final_result.json` by
(pe_mode, period), prints a summary table, writes
`results/sweep_summary.json`, and renders `results/period_sweep.png`
(test loss vs. P on a log-x axis, with baseline/RoPE as horizontal
reference bands). `tests/test_analyze_sweep.py` (3 tests) checks the
grouping/aggregation logic against synthetic final_result.json files
without requiring a real sweep or matplotlib rendering to be correct.
Manually smoke-tested end-to-end against 30 synthetic result files,
including the actual PNG render.

Full test suite: 59/59 passing.

## 2026-09-24 — More frequent progress feedback in train.py

Navid wanted regular updates on step count and current train loss for
each run, and reported not getting them regularly enough in practice.
Two separate causes:

1. The only progress line was tied to `--eval_every_steps` (100 by
   default), which also runs a real val-loss pass (10 extra forward-pass
   batches) -- expensive enough that it was kept infrequent, giving an
   update only every ~3 minutes at the real architecture's step speed.
2. Python buffers stdout in chunks (not line-by-line) when it isn't
   writing directly to a terminal -- e.g. when piped through `tee` or
   redirected to a log file -- so even those periodic lines could arrive
   in delayed bursts rather than as they're produced.

Fixed both: added `--print_every_steps` (default 10), a cheap line
(step + train_loss only, no extra compute -- the loss is already
computed for backprop) printed independently of `--eval_every_steps`,
which keeps its existing cadence for the more expensive val-loss check
and best-checkpoint tracking. When the two intervals coincide, only the
fuller eval line prints (no duplicate). Every print in train.py now
passes `flush=True` (stderr prints were already unbuffered and didn't
need it) so output appears immediately even when piped/redirected.

`tests/test_print_every_steps_decoupled_from_eval` (in
tests/test_train_protocol.py) checks the two intervals produce the
right lines at the right steps with no duplication, and that
metrics.jsonl still only logs on eval steps (not print steps) -- i.e.
the cheap prints really are free of the eval-cadence's overhead.

Full suite: 61/61 passing.

## 2026-09-25 — More frequent checkpointing + live ETA

Navid reported that interrupting the sweep felt like it lost the current
run's progress. Investigated read-only against the real paused run
(periodic_P9_seed2, 11 runs already completed) before changing anything:
its checkpoint.pt genuinely held step 652 and would resume correctly --
the mechanism wasn't broken. The real cause was the checkpoint interval
(--checkpoint_every_seconds default 600s = 10 min): interrupting more
often than that window, which is easy to do when checking in casually,
means a chunk of progress since the last checkpoint is genuinely gone
each time, which reads as "keeps starting over."

Fix: lowered --checkpoint_every_seconds default to 60s. A checkpoint
write is ~30-35MB to local SSD, a small fraction of a second against
~1.8s/step, so checkpointing 10x more often costs negligible overhead.
Verified (read-only, before and after the code change) that the paused
run's real checkpoint at step 652 is untouched and would still resume
correctly -- checkpoint format didn't change, and checkpoint_every_seconds
was already outside IDENTITY_KEYS, so this can't trigger a resume
mismatch. All 11 completed runs' final_result.json files are also
untouched (run_sweep.py's skip-by-completion logic is unchanged).

Also added, per Navid's request: a live ETA on every progress line.
train.py now prints "run ETA: ..." (from this invocation's own live
step rate since it started/resumed -- not a stale historical average)
and, when launched by run_sweep.py, "sweep ETA: ... (N run(s) left
after this)" (current run's ETA plus N x the average real duration of
runs already completed in this sweep, measured from their metrics.jsonl,
recomputed fresh before each launch so it gets more accurate as the
sweep progresses). Falls back to self-extrapolating this run's own rate
if no runs have completed yet. Both new --eta_avg_run_seconds/
--eta_runs_remaining args are display-only and deliberately excluded
from IDENTITY_KEYS.

8 new tests (test_checkpoint_every_seconds_default_is_60,
test_eta_suffix_appears_with_sweep_context,
test_eta_suffix_omits_sweep_eta_without_runs_remaining,
test_resume_unaffected_by_changing_eta_args in test_train_protocol.py;
measure/average_completed_run_seconds tests in test_run_sweep.py).

**Headline result (the core research question): periodic PE did not

sweep. Consistent with the broader literature where RoPE (relative,
multiplicative position information applied inside attention) tends to
outperform additive/concat absolute sinusoidal PE for autoregressive
LMs -- not a surprising result in itself, but a useful calibration
point: the periodic-PE effect (or lack thereof) is small next to the
base-PE-mechanism effect.

this result may reflect what periodic PE does on its own merits, without
that confound inflating large-P performance. Worth discussing explicitly

## 2026-10-01 -- Formal significance testing (scripts/stats_sweep.py)

User asked for a formal significance test on two separate questions: (1)
periodic-vs-baseline, per period, and (2) whether the period-to-period
spread visible in the plot is real or "just an artefact of our chosen
scale for the loss function." Implemented `scripts/stats_sweep.py` with
tests in `tests/test_stats_sweep.py` (14 tests, synthetic data with known
properties, all passing).

**Method.** All 10 configs share identical seeds {0,1,2} by protocol
design (same init, same data shuffle order), so every comparison is
PAIRED (`scipy.stats.ttest_rel`), not independent-samples -- this removes
seed-to-seed noise shared between the two arms being compared and gives
much more power at n=3 than an unpaired test would. For the
periodic-vs-baseline family (8 simultaneous comparisons), used
Holm-Bonferroni correction on top of the raw p-values, plus Cohen's d_z
(paired effect size) since a p-value alone is easy to misread at n=3. For
the period-vs-period question, ran two omnibus tests across the 8
periodic configs (period as within-subject factor, seed as subject):
Friedman (non-parametric, `scipy.stats.friedmanchisquare`) and
repeated-measures ANOVA (`statsmodels.stats.anova.AnovaRM`). Also a
direct paired comparison of the best (P=3) vs. worst (P=128) periodic
config.

**On "is this an artefact of scale": no.** Statistical significance is
the ratio of an effect to its measurement noise (standard error); it
doesn't depend on how a plot's y-axis happens to be drawn or zoomed. The
period-sweep plot's y-axis is compressed because it also has to
accommodate RoPE's much larger effect on the same axes -- that's a
plotting choice, not evidence about whether the period-to-period gaps
are real. The omnibus tests answer the real question directly.

**Results (metric: final_test_loss at best-val checkpoint):**

1. Periodic vs. baseline, per period (paired t-test, Holm-Bonferroni
   corrected across the 8 comparisons): NONE reach significance at
   alpha=0.05 after correction. Smallest corrected p is P=45 at
   p_holm=0.2656 (raw p=0.0332). This is despite large paired effect
   sizes in every case (Cohen's d from 1.17 to 3.09, all in the
   direction of periodic being worse than baseline) -- the effect is
   consistent in direction and sign across all 8 periods, but n=3 seeds
   with an 8-way correction simply doesn't have the power to call any
   single comparison significant. Read this as "probably a real, small,
   negative effect of periodic PE on this task, but not formally proven
   at conventional significance with this sample size" -- not as "no
   effect."

2. Omnibus period-to-period effect (within the 8 periodic configs only,
   excluding baseline/RoPE): Friedman chi-square=19.00, p=0.0082;
   repeated-measures ANOVA F(7,14)=21.62, p<0.0001. Both strongly reject
   the null that period choice has no systematic effect. **The
   period-to-period spread visible in the plot is real, not a scale
   artefact or noise** -- this directly answers the user's question.

3. Best (P=3) vs. worst (P=128) periodic config: paired t=-7.94,
   p=0.0155, Cohen's d=-4.58. Significant even after accounting for the
   small sample -- the gap between the best and worst period choices
   within the periodic family is a real, large effect, not noise.

**Net picture:** period choice clearly matters once you've committed to
periodic PE (finding 2 and 3), and periodic PE is consistently -- if not
yet individually-significantly, given n=3 and multiple-comparison
correction -- worse than baseline at every period tested (finding 1).
The headline interpretation in the prior entry (periodic PE doesn't help
and mildly hurts on this task) stands, now with the honest caveat that
the baseline comparison's significance is bounded by sample size, while
the shape of the period curve itself (the "is period 45-128 really worse
than period 2-16" question) is on much firmer statistical ground.

## 2026-10-01 -- Correction: best-vs-worst comparison was uncorrected (selection bias)

User asked me to double-check the significance-test entry above because
finding #3 (best P=3 vs. worst P=128, reported as significant at p=0.0155)
looked inconsistent with finding #1 (no individual period beats baseline
even at the LEAST conservative case, P=45 raw p=0.0332, after Holm
correction). It was inconsistent, and the inconsistency pointed at a real
bug in the method, not just presentation.

**The bug:** P=3 and P=128 were selected as "best" and "worst" by looking
at the data -- picking the single lowest- and highest-mean periods out of
8. Running an uncorrected paired t-test on a pair chosen that way is the
"testing the winner" problem: Fisher's-LSD-style single-pair tests are
only valid when the pair is chosen *before* seeing the data; for more
than 3 groups you need a correction that accounts for the full comparison
space the extreme pair could have been drawn from (this is exactly why
Tukey's HSD / the studentized range distribution exist, rather than just
running a plain t-test on whichever two group means look most different).
I had correctly Holm-corrected the periodic-vs-baseline family (8 tests)
but then ran the best-vs-worst test uncorrected -- an inconsistent
application of the same principle within one report.

**Fix (`scripts/stats_sweep.py`):** added `all_pairwise_period_comparisons`,
which runs all C(8,2)=28 period-vs-period paired t-tests and Holm-corrects
across that full family. `best_vs_worst` now reports its p-value by
looking up the best/worst pair's result *within* that corrected family
(`p_value_holm_within_all_pairs`), rather than running its own uncorrected
test. The uncorrected value is still reported alongside, explicitly
labeled as not a valid standalone significance test. Added tests verifying
the Holm correction is applied across all 28 pairs and that
`best_vs_worst`'s corrected value matches the corresponding row in the
full pairwise table.

**Corrected result:** best (P=3) vs. worst (P=128): uncorrected p=0.0155,
but Holm-corrected (within the 28-pair family) p=0.3413 -- NOT
significant. This supersedes the "significant, d=-4.58" claim in the
prior entry.

**Corrected net picture.** All three results are now mutually consistent:

1. No individual period differs significantly from baseline after
   correction (prior entry, unchanged).
2. The omnibus test across all 8 periods (Friedman p=0.0082, RM-ANOVA
   p<0.0001) still finds real, systematic period-to-period structure --
   this part of the prior entry's answer to "is it a scale artefact"
   stands: no, it isn't.
3. But once any SPECIFIC pairwise comparison is corrected for having been
   selected from the data (including literally the most extreme pair,
   best vs. worst), none reaches significance at n=3 -- not even the
   single largest gap in the whole sweep.

This is not actually contradictory, and it's a well-documented pattern:
an omnibus test pools all the data at once and can detect that *some*
real structure exists with more power than any individual corrected
pairwise comparison can resolve at small n. The honest summary is:
period choice provably matters in aggregate (2), but at n=3 seeds we
cannot say with statistical confidence *which specific periods* differ
from which, including the apparently largest gap in the data (1, 3).
Don't over-read the shape of the curve in the plot pointwise -- treat it
as suggestive, not as individually confirmed differences, until a larger
seed count is run.
